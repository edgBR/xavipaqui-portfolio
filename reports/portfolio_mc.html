Monte Carlo · 20,000 paths · Sept 2026

Two retirements,
five methods

A 60/40 portfolio for a 58-year-old and a 25/75 for a 64-year-old — contributing until 85, taking 20 % out at years 10 and 20, held to 90 — under five weighting methods and three return engines. The methods barely disagree. The engines disagree enormously — which means any ranking you read off a Monte Carlo here is the return assumption talking, not the optimiser.

Data — seven funds fetched by fetch_navs.py (FT Markets, Morningstar via mstarpy, Yahoo proxies). Covariance from the 149-month common window, 2014-04 → 2026-08. Weights from hrp_nco.py; projection from monte_carlo.py.
Cash flows — contributions run to age 85, then the capital is left alone to 90. Two withdrawals of 20 % of the then-current value land at years 10 and 20 (ages 68 & 78, and 74 & 84). They apply to every path, the lump-sum cases included, so returns here are money-weighted (IRR) rather than simple CAGR.
Not advice — this is how the pieces behave arithmetically, not a recommendation to buy anything.
Minimum vocabulary

The objects, the optimiser and the engine.

Written for someone fluent in statistics and mathematics but not in finance. The machinery will be familiar; what needs pinning down are the three or four financial terms.

The objects

A portfolio is a weight vector w on the simplex: wᵢ ≥ 0, Σwᵢ = 1. Each weight is the fraction of the money held in one fund.

The six funds' monthly returns form a random vector r with mean vector μ and covariance matrix Σ. Because a portfolio return is a linear combination, w^T r, everything else follows:

expected return = w^T μ variance = w^T Σ w

That is, literally, all the theory required. "Equity" and "bonds" are not theoretical categories — they are assets with large or small diagonal entries in Σ: 13–16 % against 1.8–6 % annualised standard deviation. Two financial terms worth pinning down:

VolatilityAnnualised standard deviation of returns. Nothing more than that.
DrawdownThe minimum of Vₜ / max(V₁…ₜ) − 1 along the path: the worst decline from a prior peak. It is the quantity that makes people sell at the worst possible moment, so it matters behaviourally and not only statistically.

The optimiser: a function (Σ, sometimes μ) → w

HRP (Hierarchical Risk Parity) uses machinery you already have. It converts correlation into a proper metric distance d = √((1−ρ)/2), builds a single-linkage dendrogram, reorders Σ to be quasi-diagonal, then allocates risk by recursively bisecting that dendrogram: at each split, each branch receives a share inversely proportional to its intra-cluster variance. It is the same procedure as clustering expression profiles from a distance matrix — only what the distance measures changes. It never touches μ.

NCO (Nested Clustered Optimization) runs k-means on that same distance matrix, choosing k by silhouette; solves minimum-variance within each cluster; then solves it again across the cluster representatives. The nesting exists to avoid inverting an ill-conditioned Σ in one step: with 6 assets and 149 observations, Σ̂⁻¹ amplifies estimation noise brutally.

The hand-set weights are not an optimiser. They are chosen by judgement about human circumstances — age, when the money is needed — that are not in the data and that no estimator can see.

Why √((1−ρ)/2) and not just 1−ρ

Correlation is a similarity, not a distance, and clustering routines assume a metric space. A metric must be non-negative, zero only for identical objects, symmetric, and obey the triangle inequality. 1−ρ satisfies the first three and fails the last, so it cannot be handed to a linkage algorithm without the hierarchy becoming arbitrary.

The transform fixes it, and the two pieces do separate jobs. The 1−ρ numerator is what inverts the sense — it turns "moves together" into "is close". The /2 and the square root are what make the result an actual metric rather than a monotone rescaling:

ρ = +1 → d = 0 (identical) ρ = 0 → d = 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 ρ = −1 → d = 1 (maximally distant)

The reason it works is that d is a genuine Euclidean distance in disguise. Standardise each return series to zero mean and unit variance; then d(i,j) is exactly 1/√(2T) times the Euclidean norm of the difference between the two standardised vectors. So the assets really do sit in a metric space, and the triangle inequality holds because it holds in ℝT.

One consequence is worth stating because it surprises people: perfectly anti-correlated assets are maximally distant here, not close. For risk clustering that is the right sign. Two assets that hedge each other belong in different branches, because putting them in the same bucket and then equalising risk within it would cancel out the very diversification you are trying to place.

What "single linkage" means, and what it costs

Agglomerative hierarchical clustering: start with every asset as its own cluster, repeatedly merge the two closest clusters, and record the distance at which each merge happened. The linkage is the rule for measuring distance between two clusters rather than two points:

singlethe minimum distance between any member of A and any member of B — nearest neighbour. This is what HRP specifies.
completethe maximum such distance — farthest neighbour. Produces compact, ball-shaped clusters.
average / Wardthe mean over all cross-pairs; Ward instead merges whichever pair increases total within-cluster variance least.

The dendrogram is the resulting merge tree: leaves are assets, each internal node is a merge, and the node's height is the distance at which it occurred. Cutting the tree at a height gives you a clustering; HRP never cuts it, it walks it.

Single linkage is equivalent to building a minimum spanning tree, which means it chains: it will connect two barely-related assets through a chain of intermediaries, producing elongated clusters rather than compact ones. It is also the linkage most sensitive to noise, because a single spuriously-close pair can merge two otherwise distinct groups. With six assets this is nearly irrelevant — the equity/bond split is far too strong to be confused. With five hundred it matters a great deal. Treat it as a documented choice from the paper, not a demonstrably optimal one.

Quasi-diagonalisation, and why Σ cares

quasi_diag() extracts the dendrogram's leaf order — the left-to-right sequence of leaves when the tree is drawn without crossing branches — and permutes the rows and columns of Σ into that order.

A matrix is quasi-diagonal when its large entries sit near the main diagonal and decay as you move away from it. That is precisely what the leaf order buys: assets that merged early (high correlation, small distance) end up adjacent, so their large covariances land next to the diagonal, while the far corners hold the near-zero cross-sleeve terms. It is a permutation only — no number in Σ changes, just the labelling of the axes.

Three reasons this matters:

It is what makes the recursive bisection meaningfulHRP allocates by splitting the ordered list in half, then in half again. That is only a sensible operation if position in the list carries information — if adjacent entries are genuinely similar. Quasi-diagonalisation is the step that makes "the first half" a real cluster rather than an arbitrary subset. Run the same bisection on an alphabetical ordering and the weights are meaningless.
It localises estimation errorA quasi-diagonal Σ is approximately block-diagonal, and a block-diagonal matrix inverts block by block. Classical Markowitz needs Σ⁻¹, and inversion mixes every entry with every other, so one badly-estimated covariance contaminates every weight. HRP never inverts anything — it only ever compares within-cluster variances of adjacent blocks. An error in one corner of Σ stays in that corner. This is the whole robustness argument, and it is a structural property, not a statistical one.
It is a free diagnosticLook at the correlation matrix printed by hrp_nco.py: World / EM / SmallCap correlate 0.65–0.92 with one another and only 0.27–0.57 with the bond funds; GlobalBond and ShortBond correlate 0.92. Under the leaf order that is two dense blocks with a sparse off-diagonal region — visibly quasi-diagonal. If it did not come out that way, the hierarchy would be telling you these assets do not cluster, and HRP's premise would not hold for them.

Why HRP and NCO discard μ

Here is the statistical argument, and it is decisive. The standard error of the annualised mean is σ/√T; that of the standard deviation is approximately σ/√(2(n−1)). With n = 149 months:

In relative terms the mean is an order of magnitude noisier than the variance. World's mean is 12.64 % with a 95 % confidence interval of [5.26 %, 20.02 %] — fifteen points wide, spanning "mediocre" to "spectacular". Narrowing it to ±1 point would take 176 years of data. The bond funds are worse still: the standard error exceeds the estimate itself, so even the sign is undetermined.

Σ, on that same data, is estimated to 6 % relative error across every asset. Hence the whole López de Prado programme: discard the hopeless estimator and keep the one that converges. An optimiser consuming only Σ is robust by construction, not by luck.

The engine: a function w → distribution of paths

The optimiser answers "which weights?". The engine answers "and then what happens?" — it is the generative model for future paths. There are three, and the distinction is exactly non-parametric versus parametric.

bootstrap Non-parametric

Politis–Romano stationary bootstrap: geometric block lengths (mean 12 months) with circular wrap-around. It resamples whole rows, so the cross-asset dependence structure is preserved empirically without estimating Σ; and because blocks are contiguous runs, some autocorrelation and volatility clustering survive too. Kurtosis and skew survive as well. The price: it cannot generate anything absent from the sample, and it inherits the sample's μ̂ without declaring it.

hist-mvn Parametric, sample moments

Lognormal returns with μ̂ and Σ̂. Gaussian tail in logs, so it understates extreme events relative to the bootstrap. It is the comparison point that isolates the effect of imposing a parametric form.

fwd-mvn Parametric, with μ replaced

Identical to the above, swapping μ̂ for an external estimate: 6 % equity, 3.3 % bonds. It is shrinkage toward a prior, justified by the table above — trading an unbiased estimator for one with far lower variance. This is the engine the headline figures on this page are read from.

Both parametric engines are moment-matched in log space. If G = exp(X) with X ~ N(m, s²), matching both moments to targets μ and sd requires:

s² = log(1 + sd² / (1+μ)²) m = log(1+μ) − s²/2

Without that adjustment a 6 % assumption silently simulates something else: the mean of a lognormal is not the exponential of its mean. This is also why the medians on this page fall below the stated assumption — the 60/40 has a 4.92 % arithmetic mean by construction but a 4.59 % median. Those 0.33 points are volatility drag, not an error.

The consequence that organises the whole page: bootstrap and hist-mvn share μ̂, so they differ only in the tails. fwd-mvn changes μ, and that dominates everything else.

Why the forward bars are flat: fwd-mvn assigns 6 % to every equity sleeve and 3.3 % to every bond sleeve, so at a fixed equity share every method has the same expected return by construction — only volatility differs, which moves the median by hundredths. How wide the outcomes are

The uncertainty inside one method is far larger than the difference between methods.

Each range is roughly 5 percentage points wide. The largest gap between any two constrained methods is under 0.1pp — about one fiftieth of the width of a single method's own uncertainty. Choosing between HRP, NCO and the hand-set weights is a rounding error against the risk actually being taken.

What the charges do

Fees are the one dimension where the weighting method actually matters.

Published fund NAVs are already net of the fund's ongoing charge, so μ̂ and Σ̂ — and therefore the bootstrap and hist-mvn engines — have charges embedded whether or not you think about them. fwd-mvn did not: 6 % and 3.3 % are gross index-level assumptions. It now subtracts each weight vector's own weighted ongoing charge.

Weighted ongoing charge by method, and its effect on the forward median

The charge spread across methods runs 0.081 % to 0.132 %/yr — a 0.051pp range, which is wider than the entire 0.05pp spread in gross expected return between the constrained methods. And unlike expected return, this difference is deterministic: it does not need 176 years of data to establish. HRP-in-sleeves is the most expensive of the three constrained methods because it loads emerging markets (0.16 %) and small caps (0.29 %) well above their hand-set weights; NCO-in-sleeves is the cheapest because it drops both small caps and the intermediate bond fund entirely. If you want a data-driven reason to prefer one optimiser here, cost is the only one that survives scrutiny — and it favours NCO by about 0.026pp/yr.

What is still not costed

Platform and custody charges are modelled as zero. That is right for MyInvestor, which levies no custody fee on funds, but wrong for platforms that do. The effect is pure compounding drag on the terminal value:

Rebalancing is assumed free. stats() rebalances monthly at zero cost. In reality a Spanish traspaso is tax-free but not instantaneous — it takes several business days out of the market, and the NAV you leave is not the NAV you arrive at. Monthly rebalancing is also more frequent than anyone would actually do; annual would be more realistic and would cost less.

The historical series carry the wrong charges. Three sleeves — World, EM and ShortBond — take their history from longer-lived proxy funds, not from the chosen S share classes. Those proxies generally charge more (the Vanguard Global Stock Index proxy runs about 0.18 % against the iShares S class's 0.06 %), so μ̂ is net of a heavier fee than the actual portfolio would pay. The bootstrap and hist-mvn engines are therefore mildly conservative on this axis — which is the safe direction to be wrong in, but it is not zero.

What would change these numbers

Four honest limits.

2008 is not in the sample

The common window starts 2014-04, because the short-bond proxy does. Every covariance, correlation and drawdown here is fitted to a period with no global financial crisis. The simulated median drawdown of about 22 % for the 60/40 is therefore the optimistic case.

The bootstrap inherits a strange decade

2014–2026 combined an exceptional equity run with the worst bond years in decades. The bootstrap engine faithfully resamples that, which is why it rates bond-heavy methods so poorly. It is the most assumption-free engine and the least representative sample — those are the same fact.

The forward engine cannot rank sleeves

Assigning one number to all equity and one to all bonds makes EM, small caps and developed world indistinguishable to it. If you want the projection to express a view on the EM or small-cap premium, that view has to be typed in as a per-sleeve expected return — at which point the forecast, not the optimiser, is doing the work.

The withdrawal schedule is stylised

20 % at years 10 and 20 is a round assumption, not a spending plan. Two things follow from its shape. Taking a percentage is self-limiting — the pot scales down but never empties — whereas a fixed euro withdrawal can exhaust it, and is far more exposed to a bad sequence of early returns. And two lumps a decade apart is not how retirement spending actually behaves; a monthly stream would sample market conditions far more often and would carry meaningfully more sequence risk. These numbers are therefore the gentler case.

Carmignac is fetched but excluded

Its NAV history is now in the CSV (93 months, from 2018-12), but it is not in either sleeve. Its realised 8.0 % volatility and −13.8 % worst month put it closer to equity than to ballast, and including it would truncate the common window from 149 months to 93.
